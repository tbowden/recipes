    Focaccia

    This recipe can be warm or cold fermented depending on time constraints. The quantities suit a baking dish of about 30 X 35 cm. Near enough is good enough. All percentages are in baker's percentage. As this is a high hydration recipe it will produce a sticky dough, but with a bit of practice it can be worked with. Don't use extra flour to deal with the stickiness. Instead, lightly oiling your hands and/or bench. This makes it much easier to work with the dough.

    Ingredients

    • 500g bread flour...

      I use Laucke Wallaby flour as it has the highest protein level of any of the flour varieties available from the supermarket.

    • 375g water (75% hydration)

      Focaccia likes to have a high hydration dough. I started with 80% hydration but the protein content of this flour isn't high enough for that so I dropped the hydration back a bit. As it is, it is still on the upper end of what this flour can do. YMMV.

    • 10g salt (2%)

    • 3g instant dried yeast (or ~9g fresh yeast)

      If you want to speed up the fermentation you can increase the yeast but you will have less margin for error as the fermentation reaches critical stages. A faster fermentation also gives less time for flavour development.

    • 20g olive oil

    For toppings

    • 30 - 50g olive oil

    • Sprinkle of flaky salt (I use freshly ground rock salt which supposedly is not quite as good)

    • Sun dried tomato pieces (optional but worthwile if you like that sort of thing)

    • Fresh (or dried) rosemary (otional but again, worthwile)

    • Other toppings are possible (various varieties of cheese for example) once you've got the basics working

    Method

    • Initial mix of flour and water till combined (mixer or by hand) then cover

    • Autolyse rest for 20 - 60 minutes (improves gluted development)

    • Mix in salt and yeast for a minute or two and then oil until fully incorporated

      The oil will make the dough sloppy and it will struggle to incorporate initially but after about 3-5 minutes on a medium speed it will finally incorporate. This is much easier by machine than by hand.

    • Bulk fermentation (approx 2 - 4 hours but variable depending on temperature and yeast quantity) with strech and fold every 20-30 minutes for the first 90 minutes

      The stretch and folds are the main way of developing gluten while giving time for flavour development. As the stretch and folds progress you should notice the dough gaining strength. In between, the gluten will get a chance to relax. You are developing structure without the heavy kneading that is normally done. The dough will become smoother, airier and more elastic during this process. The primary indicator for the end of this stage is not the clock but the dough. It should have increased in volume by about 50-75%. The dough should feel gassier and lighter than at the start. If you pull it, it should feel light and airy rather than tight. The poke test: When pressed with your finger, the dough spring back partly but slowly. If it fully springs back quickly it is under fermented and needs more time. If it doesn't spring back at all, it is over fermented and cannot be fixed. If you are doing a cold ferment stage, it is better to err on the side of slight under fermentation rather than over fermentation.

    • Cold fermentation (12-24hrs) - cover the dough and let it rest in the fridge (about 4°C)

      This step is optional but enhances flavour development. The dough will continue to ferment, but much more slowly.

    • Proofing: Transfer the dough to a well oiled pan (approx 25x30cm) and gently tease it out to cover most of the pan Be generous with the oil. The bottom of the focaccia needs to almost fry in the oil. You may need several goes at spreading out the dough, especially if you have done a cold ferment and it is still tight. You want to gently spread the dough without tearing it or knocking out gas. Let it go at it's own pace. You will prolly need to let it relax for a few minutes in between attempts. The dough should approximately double in size in the pan in this stage. The real test though is it is ready when the dough is bubbly and wobbles like jelly when you gently shake the pan.

    • First dimpling: do the first set of dimples with your fingers when you initially spread the dough in the pan

      Don't be timid with this. Push your fingers right to the bottom of the pan. It will help if you oil your fingers so the dough doesn't stick.

    • Second dimpling once the proofing is done Dimple the dough again and add the salt along with any other toppings. Be generous with putting oil on the top. It will pool in the dimples and help give the typical focaccia finish.

    • Bake for 230 - 250c for 20 - 30 minutes. Bake until it is a deep golden brown with a crisp underside and visibly fried edges from the olive oil

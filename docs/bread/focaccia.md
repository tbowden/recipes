# Focaccia 

This recipe can be warm or cold fermented depending on time constraints. The quantities suit a baking dish of about 20 X 30 cm. Near enough is good enough. All percentages are in baker's percentage.
As this is a high hydration recipe it will produce a sticky dough, but with a bit of practice it can be worked with. Don't use extra flour to deal with the stickiness. Instead, lightly oiling your hands and/or bench.

## Ingredients

* [ ] 500g bread flour

    I use Laucke Wallaby flour as it has the highest protein level of any of the flour varieties available from the supermarket.

* [ ] 375g water (75% hydration)

    Focaccia likes to have a high hydration dough. I started with 80% hydration but the protein content of this flour isn't high enough for that so I dropped the hydration back a bit. As it is, it is still on the upper end of what this flour can do. YMMV.

* [ ] 10g salt (2%)

* [ ] 3g instant dried yeast (or ~9g fresh yeast)

* [ ] 20g olive oil

For toppings on the tray before baking:

* [ ] 30 - 50g olive oil

* [ ] Sprinkle of flaky salt (I use freshly ground rock salt which supposedly is not quite as good)

* [ ] Sun dried tomato pieces (optional but worthwile if you like that sort of thing)

* [ ] Fresh (or dried) rosemary (otional but again, worthwile)

* [ ] Other toppings are possible (various varieties of cheese for example) once you've got the basics working


## Method

* [ ] Initial mix of flour and water till combined (mixer or by hand) then cover with an autolyse rest for 20 - 60 minutes (improves gluted development)

* [ ] Mix in salt & yeast for 3-4 minutes then oil until fully incorporated

* [ ] Bulk fermentation - 2 - 4 hours (or more depending on ambient temperature - normal dough development characteristics should be observed rather than strict adherance to times). During the first 90 minutes, do a couple of stretch and folds every 20 - 30 minutes to develop gluten strength. You will feel the dough changing as you do this. With practice, you will feel the dough developing surface tension as you do your stretch and folds. In between, the gluten will get a chance to relax. You are developing structure without the heavy kneading that is normally done. The dough will become smoother, airier and more elastic during this process.

* [ ] If doing a cold ferment, transfer to fridge for 12 - 24 hours. This will slow yeast fermentation but develop more complex flavours.

* [ ] Gently transfer dough to well oiled baking tray for final fermetation. You don't want to knock out too much gas at this point. As the dough warms, gently tease out the dough to fill the tray as it relaxes. You will likely have to let the dough rest for a few minutes at a time as you do this rather than forcing the dough. Let the dough go at it's own pace. Final fermentation may take several hours if you haven't done a cold ferment. Ideally you want the dough to hold it's form but be nice and wobbly like jelly. It should have risen by about double by the end of this stage.

* [ ] Gently dimple the dough with your fingers to the bottom of the baking pan. Gently add oil, salt, and other toppings.

* [ ] Bake for 230 - 250c for 20 - 30 minutes. Bake until it is a deep golden brown with a crisp underside and visibly fried edges from the olive oil.
